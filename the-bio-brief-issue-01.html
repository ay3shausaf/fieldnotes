How an octopus "talks" without ever saying a word

Octopuses can shift their entire skin color, texture, and pattern in under a second — and for a long time, biologists assumed this was purely about hiding from predators. New research is building a stronger case that a lot of this color-shifting is actually communication: signals aimed at other octopuses, not camouflage aimed at a predator's eyes at all.

The color changes come from three layered structures in the skin. Chromatophores are tiny sacs of pigment surrounded by muscle — when the muscle contracts, the sac stretches and the color becomes visible; when it relaxes, the color shrinks out of sight. Underneath those sit iridophores, which reflect light to create shimmering blues and greens, and leucophores, which scatter light to produce white. Together, the three layers work like a screen with millions of individually controlled pixels, all wired directly to the nervous system.

What convinced researchers this was social signaling rather than just camouflage was timing and context: octopuses were observed flashing specific patterns during encounters with other octopuses — before mating, before conflict, or while retreating — patterns that made no sense as camouflage since there was no predator around, and no matching background to blend into.

Researchers are now filming these interactions frame-by-frame and cataloguing which patterns show up in which situations, essentially trying to build the first rough "dictionary" of a language made entirely of color, texture, and posture — no sound involved at all.

The Mission
Diagram: single-cell sequencing workflow

Mapping every cell type in the human body

The human body is built from roughly 37 trillion cells, and for most of medical history, biologists have only had rough categories to describe them — "muscle cell," "skin cell," "immune cell" — without knowing how many distinct sub-types actually exist inside each category.

The Human Cell Atlas is an international collaboration trying to change that: using a technique called single-cell sequencing, researchers can now read the active genes inside one individual cell at a time, across millions of cells, and use that data to sort them into precise, distinct types rather than broad guesses.

The payoff is direct: many diseases, including cancers and autoimmune conditions, are now understood to involve very specific cell sub-types misbehaving — so a complete map makes it possible to design treatments that target exactly the right cells instead of broadly affecting healthy ones too.

Axolotl

Ambystoma mexicanum

A salamander that never grows up — it keeps its juvenile gills and stays aquatic for its entire life instead of developing lungs like most amphibians, a trait called neoteny.

Its most famous ability is regeneration: an axolotl can regrow an entire lost limb, complete with bone, muscle, and nerves, over the course of a few weeks — and can also regenerate parts of its heart, spinal cord, and even brain tissue with minimal scarring.

It's critically endangered in the wild, surviving naturally only in the canal system of Lake Xochimilco near Mexico City, even though it's one of the most widely studied animals in regeneration research worldwide.
